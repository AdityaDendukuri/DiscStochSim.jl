\begin{abstract}
This paper introduces an adaptive Finite State Projection (FSP) algorithm for numerical simulation of the Chemical Master Equation (CME) over discrete state spaces. The proposed methodology focuses on multiscale/stiff systems; specifically systems that operate with vastly varying reaction rates over long time periods. Systems with stiff properties require careful management of time-stepping and state space control in the context of adaptive FSP methods. The proposed method uses the system's instantaneous flux as a proxy measure for determining the relevant states for truncation as well as the magnitude of time stepping. We demonstrate that the proposed methodology identifies important states that are critical for maintaining system connectivity and the adaptive time stepping accounts for adjusting the timestep for stiff and transient phases of the simulation.
\end{abstract}

  chemical master equation, finite state projection, adaptive methods, stiff stochastic systems, multiscale modeling, flux-based pruning
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}

The Chemical Master Equation (CME) describes the time evolution of probability distributions over discrete reaction networks and provides the standard model for intrinsic noise in biochemical kinetics \cite{Gillespie1977, Wilkinson2006-so}. Direct solution is rarely feasible because the state space is countably infinite. An important observation for practical biochemical systems is the natural sparsity of their probability distributions—most of the theoretically infinite state space carries zero or negligible probability. The Finite State Projection (FSP) method \cite{Munsky2006} exploits this sparsity by restricting the CME to a finite subset of states that collectively hold most of the probability mass. Since then, there have been several optimizations and extensions of this method~\cite{Kazeev2014, Vo2017, Dinh_2020,Sunkara2012, Jahnke2010, Burrage2006AKF, Sidje2015}, and we direct the reader to \cite{Dinh2016} for a comprehensive review of the methods. 

Biochemical reaction networks frequently exhibit extreme stiffness \cite{zhang_cao}, with reaction rates differing by many orders of magnitude over long time intervals. For example, the Robertson autocatalytic system \cite{Robertson1967} is a standard benchmark for stiff solvers with rate constants spanning nine orders of magnitude ($k_2/k_1 \approx 10^9$). Such stiffness creates two coupled challenges for adaptive FSP methods. Spatially, states with negligible probability but high exit rates may carry substantial flux and serve as critical connections between metastable regions. Temporally, system activity can vary by orders of magnitude, requiring time steps that adapt to instantaneous flux levels. Traditional probability-based pruning fails to address either challenge, removing important low-probability states and using fixed time-stepping rules.

Prior work has addressed these issues in different ways. The original FSP method \cite{Munsky2006} introduced fixed truncation with error bounds based on boundary flux. Munsky and Khammash \cite{MUNSKY2007818} extended this to time stepping variants that expand the state space at each step. Peles et al.~\cite{peles} combined FSP with time scale separation techniques to exploit multiple time scales in the generator matrix, achieving model reduction through eigenvalue decomposition. This approach requires identifying and separating fast and slow reactions a priori, which is difficult without clear time scale separation. MacNamara et al.~\cite{shev} introduced operator splitting methods and showed that CME solvers can efficiently detect equilibrium and compute moments, including hybrid FSP–leap methods for the toggle switch and other bistable systems. Wolf et al.~\cite{Wolf2010} proposed sliding-window methods that track probability movement using deterministic bounds, enabling efficient handling of unbounded state spaces. Zhang and Cao \cite{zhang_cao} introduced relative flux thresholds, retaining states whose flux exceeds a fixed fraction of the maximum flux. However, relative thresholds can miss important states when flux is broadly distributed. Kuntz et al.~\cite{kuntz2010} used the stationary flux graph to identify essential edges, providing theoretical guarantees but requiring computation of the stationary distribution. For reaction networks with conserved quantities, The \textit{slack reactant} method \cite{slack} exploit stoichiometric constraints to reduce the effective dimensionality of the state space, pruning states that violate conservation laws.

This paper introduces a flux-based adaptive FSP method that addresses both spatial and temporal stiffness. We control total boundary flux rather than relative thresholds, which gives computable error bounds without requiring stationary information. The approach adapts to time-varying dynamics where stationary measures may not exist or may not be relevant. The time step rule uses total system flux to track instantaneous activity and automatically adjusts step sizes across multiple time scales. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews the CME and FSP framework. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the flux-based pruning rule and the adaptive time step selection. Section~\ref{sec:theory} gives the error analysis. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports numerical experiments. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes with a summary and outlook.


\section{Background and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:background}

We first briefly review the necessary definitions and methods, then state the main motivation of this paper in remark~\ref{rem:stiffness-challenges}. 

\subsection{The Chemical Master Equation}

\begin{definition}[Chemical Master Equation (CME)]
The \textit{Chemical Master Equation (CME)} describes the time evolution of the probability distribution $p(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$ over the state $\boldsymbol{x}$. For a single state $\boldsymbol{x}$, the CME is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt} p(\boldsymbol{x}, t) =
    \sum_{k=1}^{M} \big[\alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\nu}_k)\, p(\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\nu}_k, t)
    - \alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x})\, p(\boldsymbol{x}, t)\big],
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x})$ is the propensity for reaction $k$ at state $\boldsymbol{x}$ and $\boldsymbol{\nu}_k$ is its stoichiometric change vector.

For the entire state space $\mathbf{X}$, the CME can be written in matrix form as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\, \boldsymbol{p}(\mathbf{X}, t) = \mathbf{A}\, \boldsymbol{p}(\mathbf{X}, t),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{p}(\mathbf{X}, t)$ is the probability vector over $\mathbf{X}$ and the \textit{generator matrix} $\mathbf{A}$ has entries
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{A}_{ij} =
    \begin{cases}
        -\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M} \alpha_k(\mathbf{X}_j), & \text{if } i=j, \\
         \alpha_k(\mathbf{X}_j) & \text{if }\, \mathbf{X}_i=\mathbf{X}_j+\boldsymbol{\nu}_k, \\
    \end{cases}
\end{align}
and $\mathbf{A}_{ij}=0$ when no reaction connects $\mathbf{X}_j$ to $\mathbf{X}_i$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Generator matrix properties]
The generator $\mathbf{A}$ satisfies:  
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\mathbf{A}_{ij}\ge 0$ for $i\neq j$ (off-diagonal nonnegative),
    \item $\mathbf{A}_{ii}\le 0$ (diagonal nonpositive),
    \item $\sum_{i} \mathbf{A}_{ij}=0$ for all $j$ (each column sums to zero),
    \item $\mathbf{A}$ has positive diagonals with zero column sums.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Finite State Projection Method}

\begin{definition}[Finite State Projection]
The \textit{Finite State Projection (FSP)} method approximates the CME by restricting dynamics to a finite subset $\mathbf{X}_J\subset\mathbf{X}$. If $\mathbf{A}_J$ is the sub-generator on $\mathbf{X}_J$ and $\boldsymbol{p}_J(0)$ is the initial distribution restricted to $\mathbf{X}_J$, then
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{p}(\mathbf{X}_J, t) \approx e^{\mathbf{A}_J t}\, \boldsymbol{p}_J(0).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Classical FSP bounds \cite{Munsky2006}]
\label{thm:classical_fsp}
Let $A$ be a CME generator (\,$A_{ij}\ge 0$ for $i\neq j$, $A_{ii}\le 0$, and $\mathbf{1}^\top A=0^\top$\,), and let $A_J$ be the principal submatrix on a finite set $J\subset \mathbf{X}$. Denote by $p_J(t)$ the full CME solution restricted to $J$ and by $e^{A_J t}p_J(0)$ the truncated solution that ignores transitions to $J'$.

If for some $t_f\ge 0$ and $\varepsilon>0$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{1}^\top e^{A_J t_f}\,p_J(0) \;\ge\; 1-\varepsilon,
\label{eq:fsp-mass-cond}
\end{equation}
then the following elementwise bounds hold:
\begin{equation}
e^{A_J t_f}\,p_J(0) \;\le\; p_J(t_f) \;\le\; e^{A_J t_f}\,p_J(0) + \varepsilon\,\mathbf{1}.
\label{eq:fsp-elementwise}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\subsubsection{Time-stepping and sliding window approaches}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{lv_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Visualization of adaptive FSP algorithm for a given time step in Algorithm~\ref{alg:fspts}. The top row plots the truncation boundary during the three phases (expand, evolve and prune) in a time step, and the bottom row plots the probability distribution.}
    \label{fig:adapt-FSP}
\end{figure}
For simulations over long time periods, maintaining a single fixed projection space becomes impractical as the reachable state space combinatorially expands as time progresses. A natural remedy is to partition the time interval $[0, T]$ into subintervals $[t_k, t_{k+1}]$ and solve a sequence of restricted problems, each on a tailored projection space $\mathbf{X}_J^{(k)}$. At each time step, the method initializes $\boldsymbol{p}_J^{(k)}(t_k)$ from the previous solution, expands $\mathbf{X}_J^{(k)}$ to capture newly reachable states, and prunes states whose probability has decayed below a threshold. 

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{FSP with Time Stepping and State Space Control}
\label{alg:fsp-naive}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE initial state $x_0$, time range $[t_0,t_f]$, propensities $\{\alpha_k(x)\}$, stoichiometries $\{\nu_k\}$, step $\Delta t$
\STATE $t \gets t_0$, \; $J \gets \{x_0\}$, \; $\boldsymbol{p} \gets [1]$
\WHILE{$t < t_f$}
  \STATE \textbf{Expand:} add neighbors of $J$ reachable by one (or preset $r$) reactions
  \STATE \textbf{Assemble:} build $\mathbf{A}_{JJ}$ on $J$
  \STATE \textbf{Evolve:} $\boldsymbol{p} \gets \exp(\mathbf{A}_{JJ}\Delta t)\,\boldsymbol{p}$
  \STATE \textbf{Prune:} remove states with $p(x)$ below a threshold, then renormalize $\boldsymbol{p}$
  \STATE $t \gets \min\{t+\Delta t,\,t_f\}$
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \textbf{return} $(J,\boldsymbol{p})$ at $t_f$
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:fspts}
\end{algorithm}



This \textit{sliding window} approach maintains computational tractability by focusing resources on the currently relevant portion of state space. Several strategies have been developed to control the size and composition of the projection space but they follow the general skeleton shown in algorithm \ref{alg:fspts}, and each time step visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:adapt-FSP}. We adopt the time-stepping framework  following remark establishes the the main goal of this paper:

\begin{remark}[Stiffness and multiscale challenges in adaptive FSP]
\label{rem:stiffness-challenges}
Adaptive FSP methods must contend with stiffness that arises when the generator $\mathbf{A}$ exhibits a wide separation of timescales across the state space, a characteristic of many biochemical reaction networks. This stiffness manifests in two coupled ways. First, \textit{spatial stiffness} emerges through connectivity bottlenecks: as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:bottleneck}, a bridge state $s_b$ with negligible probability but large exit rate $w(s_b)$ creates a fast pathway between slow metastable regions, and pruning $s_b$ based on probability alone severs this connection, rendering the generator reducible and trapping probability mass in isolated components. Second, stiffness also manifests itself temprally: when probability rapidly redistributes during transient phases, the total flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t) = \sum_{\boldsymbol{x}} p(\boldsymbol{x},t) w(\boldsymbol{x})$ becomes large and requires small steps $\Delta t$ for stability, while metastable quiescent periods with low flux permit large steps for efficiency (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-stiffness}). These manifestations are intimately coupled as the same high exit rates $w(\boldsymbol{x})$ that create spatial bottlenecks also drive temporal flux variations. Our objective is to design pruning and time-stepping heuristics that jointly address both challenges, preserving network connectivity through flux-aware state selection while adapting $\Delta t$ to track the instantaneous activity level $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t)$, thereby maintaining both structural integrity and computational tractability for robust multiscale simulation.

\section{Methodology: Flux-Based Adaptive FSP}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section introduces a state space adaptation approach that preserves network connectivity through flux-aware truncation. We first define and characterize probability flux as a connectivity measure, demonstrate that naive probability-based pruning can destroy essential pathways, then construct an algorithm that maintains structural integrity of the state-space graph while controlling computational cost.

\begin{definition}[Outgoing probability flux]
\label{def:flux}
Let $w(\boldsymbol{x}) := \sum_{k=1}^M \alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x}) = -\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}}$ denote the total exit rate from state $\boldsymbol{x}$. The \textit{outgoing probability flux} from state $\boldsymbol{x}$ at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},t) := p(\boldsymbol{x},t) \cdot w(\boldsymbol{x}).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The flux $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ measures the instantaneous rate at which probability mass exits state $\boldsymbol{x}$. For small $\Delta t > 0$, approximately $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},t) \cdot \Delta t$ units of probability mass leave $\boldsymbol{x}$ during the interval $[t, t+\Delta t]$. Crucially, states with large exit rates $w(\boldsymbol{x})$ but low probabilities $p(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ can maintain significant flux, acting as critical connections for probability flow between regions of state space despite being rarely occupied.

\begin{definition}[Total system flux]
The total probability flux of the system at time $t$ over state set $J$ is
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(J,t) := \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in J} \Phi(\boldsymbol{x},t) = \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in J} p(\boldsymbol{x},t) \cdot w(\boldsymbol{x}).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Total flux properties]
\label{prop:total-flux}
For the full state space $\mathcal{X}$ and any time $t \geq 0$, the total system flux satisfies
\begin{equation}
\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathcal{X},t) = \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{X}} p(\boldsymbol{x},t) \cdot w(\boldsymbol{x}) = -\boldsymbol{p}(t)^\top \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{A}).
\end{equation}
Since $\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}} p(\boldsymbol{x},t) = 1$ for all $t$, the total flux represents a weighted average of exit rates across the state space. For reaction networks with mass-action or similar kinetics where exit rates grow at most polynomially with state values, $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathcal{X},t)$ remains bounded on compact time intervals and tracks the instantaneous activity level of the system.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By definition of the generator matrix,
\begin{align}
\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathcal{X},t) &= \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{X}} p(\boldsymbol{x},t) \cdot (-\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}}) = -\boldsymbol{p}(t)^\top \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{A}).
\end{align}
Since the diagonal entries $\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}} = -\sum_{k=1}^M \alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x})$ depend only on the reaction network structure and state $\boldsymbol{x}$, the total flux evolves with the probability distribution. 
\end{proof}

\subsection{Flux and Network Connectivity}
\label{sec:flux-connectivity}

Having defined flux, we now establish its connection to the structural properties of the generator matrix that govern long-time behavior. A generator $\mathbf{A}$ on a finite state space $J$ is \textit{irreducible} if the corresponding state-space graph is strongly connected. Equivalently (with our column convention, where $A_{x,y}>0$ is a transition $y\!\to\!x$), there is no nontrivial partition $J=J_1\cup J_2$ with $J_1\cap J_2=\emptyset$ such that $A_{J_1,J_2}=0$ (no transitions from $J_2$ into $J_1$). Irreducibility ensures that the chain can reach any state from any other in finite expected time and implies a unique stationary distribution $\boldsymbol{\pi}\gg 0$ for the finite CTMC. The spectral gap
\[
\lambda := \min\{\operatorname{Re}(-\lambda_k(\mathbf{A})):\lambda_k(\mathbf{A})\neq 0\} > 0
\]
governs exponential convergence to stationarity (e.g., in $\pi$-weighted norms), i.e., $\|\boldsymbol{p}(t)-\boldsymbol{\pi}\|\le C e^{-\lambda t}$. The connection between flux and connectivity is formalized through Cheeger's inequality for continuous-time Markov chains \cite{Diaconis1991,63529}. Define the \textit{conductance} of a subset $S \subset J$ as
\begin{equation}
\Psi(S) := \frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in S}\sum_{\boldsymbol{y} \in J \setminus S} \pi(\boldsymbol{x}) \mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x}}}{\min\{\pi(S), \pi(J\setminus S)\}},
\end{equation}
where $\pi(S) := \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in S} \pi(\boldsymbol{x})$. Let $\Psi_* := \min_{S: 0 < \pi(S) \leq 1/2} \Psi(S)$ denote the minimum conductance, which quantifies the flux across the most restrictive bottleneck in the state-space graph. Cheeger's inequality establishes the two-sided bound
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cheeger}
\frac{\Psi_*^2}{2} \leq \lambda \leq 2\Psi_*,
\end{equation}
linking the spectral gap to the minimum conductance. Removing high-flux bridge states reduces $\Psi_*$, which by \eqref{eq:cheeger} degrades $\lambda$ and slows convergence. In the extreme case where all paths between two regions are severed, $\Psi_*=0$ and irreducibility is lost entirely. The key insight is that a bridge state can have $p(\boldsymbol{x}_b,t)\ll 1$ yet large flux $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_b,t)=p(\boldsymbol{x}_b,t)\,w(\boldsymbol{x}_b)$ when its exit rate $w(\boldsymbol{x}_b)=-A_{\boldsymbol{x}_b,\boldsymbol{x}_b}$ is large. In an adaptive FSP approach we replace one very large sparse generator by a sequence of smaller truncated generators. To keep long-time behavior stable across this sequence, we want the spectral gap to remain nearly invariant under truncation. This means we must avoid pruning states whose removal collapses the gap. Flux provides a concrete signal for this: high-flux bridge states should not be truncated.

\subsubsection{Flux-Aware Truncation Algorithm}

We propose a flux based truncation approach that protects low-probability states which carry high probability flow and are essential for connectivity. Consider a bottleneck state $s_b$ connecting two metastable regions A and B (Fig.~\ref{fig:bottleneck}). Even if $p(s_b, t) \sim 10^{-6}$ is very small, the state can carry substantial probability flux when its exit rate is large: $w(s_b) = -A_{s_b,s_b} \sim 10^9$, yielding $\Phi(s_b) = p(s_b) w(s_b) \sim 10^3$. In the CME/FSP setting, the object of interest is the full probability distribution and its flow across the state-space graph. High $\Phi(s_b)$ means $s_b$ transmits a non-negligible share of the total probability transfer between regions, so removing it degrades conductance and shrinks the spectral gap, which in turn harms mixing and long-time accuracy. By contrast, in SSA the statistic is a single trajectory: rare states like $s_b$ are visited infrequently and their role is detected only through many trajectories or long-time sampling, so per-step flux is not directly observable from a single path. This difference explains why flux is a reliable signal for pruning in CME/FSP (where ensemble transport matters) but offers limited guidance for a single-trajectory simulator.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Flux-Protected Pruning}
\label{alg:flux-prune}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Active set $\mathcal{S}$, probability vector $\boldsymbol{p}$, generator $\mathbf{A}$, quantile tolerance $\alpha \in (0,1)$, flux tolerance $\epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}} > 0$
\ENSURE Updated set $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}$ and renormalized probability $\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{new}}$
\STATE
\STATE \textit{Stage 1: Candidate Selection}
\STATE Sort states $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{S}$ in ascending order of $p(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$
\STATE Define candidate set $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ as the minimal set satisfying
\begin{equation}
\sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{C}} p(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \leq \alpha \quad \text{and} \quad p(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \leq p(\boldsymbol{y}, t) \; \forall \boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{C}, \boldsymbol{y} \in \mathcal{S} \setminus \mathcal{C}
\end{equation}
\STATE \textit{Stage 2: Flux-Based Protection}
\FOR{each $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{S}$}
    \STATE Compute exit rate: $w(\boldsymbol{x}) = -\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}}$
    \STATE Compute flux: $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}, t) = p(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \cdot w(\boldsymbol{x})$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Compute total flux: $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \Phi(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$
\STATE Set flux threshold: $\Phi_{\mathrm{thr}} = \epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}} \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}$
\STATE Define protected set: $\mathcal{P} = \{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{C} : \Phi(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \geq \Phi_{\mathrm{thr}}\}$
\STATE Define pruning set: $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{prune}} = \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{P}$
\STATE
\STATE \textit{Stage 3: Update and Renormalization}
\STATE $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \mathcal{S} \setminus \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{prune}}$
\STATE $\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \boldsymbol{p}|_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}} / \|\boldsymbol{p}|_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}}\|_1$ \quad (restrict and renormalize)
\RETURN $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}, \boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{new}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

 Stage 1 identifies low-probability candidate states for removal based on the quantile threshold $\alpha$. Stage 2 protects states in $\mathcal{C}$ that carry significant flux relative to the total system flux, preventing the removal of bottleneck pathways. Stage 3 removes unprotected candidates and renormalizes the probability distribution. The flux tolerance $\epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}}$ controls the trade-off between compression (smaller $\mathcal{S}$) and connectivity preservation (retaining critical pathways).

\subsection{Adaptive Time Stepping}

Having established that flux preservation maintains connectivity (via Cheeger's inequality in Section~\ref{sec:flux-connectivity}), we now address the temporal aspect of stiffness discussed in Remark~\ref{rem:stiffness-challenges}. The time step must adapt to the system's instantaneous activity level to maintain both stability and efficiency. Our approach is inspired by tau-leaping methods for stochastic simulation \cite{gillespie2001approximate,cao2006efficient      }, which choose time steps based on the total propensity (reaction rate) to limit the expected number of events per step. For the CME, the analogous quantity is the total system flux $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathcal{S}_n, t_n) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{S}_n} p(x, t_n) w(x)$, which measures the instantaneous rate of probability redistribution. In stiff ODE terminology \cite{Hairer1996}, this flux controls the characteristic timescale of the fastest dynamics currently active in the truncated system. To control the flux-induced local error, we choose the time step as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:flux-timestep}
\Delta t_n = \frac{\epsilon_{\Delta t}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathcal{S}_n, t_n)},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_{\Delta t}$ is a dimensionless tolerance parameter controlling the maximum probability mass allowed to cross the boundary per step. This rule ensures that $\Delta t_n \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n) \approx \epsilon_{\Delta t}$, limiting the total flux activity during each step. The formal justification for this choice follows from the local error analysis in Section~\ref{sec:theory}, where we show that the flux-induced error $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_n$ is proportional to $\Delta t_n \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n)$. The flux-adaptive approach bears close resemblance to adaptive time-stepping methods for stiff ODEs, which adjust step sizes based on local Lipschitz constants or spectral radius estimates \cite{Hairer1996,Shampine1997}. Here, $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}$ serves as a readily computable proxy for the dominant timescale. Unlike traditional stiffness estimators that require eigenvalue computations or Jacobian evaluations, the total flux is directly available from the probability distribution and propensity functions at negligible computational cost. 
\begin{remark}
    In practice, we bound the time step within the interval $[\Delta t_{\min}, \Delta t_{\max}]$ to prevent excessively small steps during brief transients and to avoid missing important dynamics during extended quiescent periods. Additionally, when the state space is expanded or pruned, we recompute $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}$ with the updated probability distribution before selecting the next time step.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Case Study: Reaction System With a Bottleneck Channel}

Left: means; center: active set size; right: final marginals at $t=10^8$ s.}

We consider a three-species network with a slow gateway followed by fast downstream dynamics:
\[
A \xrightarrow{k_1} B, \qquad
B \xrightarrow{k_2} B + C,
\]
with state $(N_A,N_B,N_C)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^3$, $k_1=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, $k_2=1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, and initial condition $(1,0,0)$. The intermediate $(0,1,0)$ is a bottleneck: it has tiny probability but high exit rate $w=k_2$, so it can carry non-negligible flux. We simulate to $T=10^8$ s using quantile-based pruning (lowest 90\% of probability mass marked as candidates) with and without flux protection (Fig.~\ref{fig:bottleneck_results}). The flux-aware variant protects any candidate with $\Phi(x)=p(x)w(x)\ge 10^{-4}\,\Phi_{\text{total}}$. In the probability-only run, $(0,1,0)$ is pruned immediately, the truncated generator becomes reducible with $|\mathcal S|=1$, means stay constant, and the final distribution places unit mass on $(1,0,0)$. With flux-aware pruning, $(0,1,0)$ is retained, irreducibility is preserved, and the full dynamics appear: $N_A$ decays slowly, $N_B$ is transient, and $N_C$ grows. The transition timescale is $\tau\sim k_1^{-1}=10^6$ s, so dynamics remain mostly quiescent until $t\approx 10^7$ s when the expected number of $A\!\to\!B$ events becomes $O(1)$ and autocatalysis of $C$ begins. By $t=10^8$ s the active set has expanded to $|\mathcal S|\approx 50$, and the final mass concentrates near $N_A=0$, $N_B=0$, $N_C>0$.


\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

We derive local and global error bounds for the adaptive FSP method based on probability fluxes across the truncation boundary. Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote the full state space and $J \subset \mathcal{X}$ be a finite active set. Define the complement $J' := \mathcal{X} \setminus J$ with $J \cap J' = \emptyset$ and $J \cup J' = \mathcal{X}$. The generator matrix admits a natural block decomposition:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:block-partition}
\mathbf{A}=
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf{A}_{JJ} & \mathbf{A}_{JJ'}\\
\mathbf{A}_{J'J} & \mathbf{A}_{J'J'}
\end{pmatrix}, 
\qquad
\boldsymbol{p}(t)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\boldsymbol{p}_J(t)\\
\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t)
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}_{JJ} \in \mathbb{R}^{|J| \times |J|}$ governs transitions within $J$, $\mathbf{A}_{J'J'} \in \mathbb{R}^{|J'| \times |J'|}$ governs transitions within $J'$, $\mathbf{A}_{JJ'} \in \mathbb{R}^{|J| \times |J'|}$ describes influx from $J'$ to $J$, and $\mathbf{A}_{J'J} \in \mathbb{R}^{|J'| \times |J|}$ describes outflux from $J$ to $J'$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:transitions}). The partitioned CME becomes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:partitioned-cme}
\frac{d}{dt}
\begin{pmatrix}
\boldsymbol{p}_J(t) \\
\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf{A}_{JJ} & \mathbf{A}_{JJ'}\\
\mathbf{A}_{J'J} & \mathbf{A}_{J'J'}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\boldsymbol{p}_J(t) \\
\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t)
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where, 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:row-sum-identities}
\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ} = -\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{J'J}, \qquad \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{J'J'} = -\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ'}.
\end{equation}
The column sum property of the generator $\mathbf{A}$ immediately yields the identities in Eq.~\eqref{eq:row-sum-identities}.

We define the boundary flux rates as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:flux-defs}
\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t) := \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{J'J}\boldsymbol{p}_J(t), \qquad \Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(t) := \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ'}\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t)$ is the total probability mass per unit time flowing from $J$ to $J'$, and $\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(t)$ is the mass flowing from $J'$ to $J$. Both quantities are non-negative. The original FSP approach defines an absorbing sink state that collapses the complement of the truncated set. Following the block formulation, it is equivalent to the \textit{compressed generator} formed by adding the outgoing probability flux back to the diagonal elements:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:compressed-generator}
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ} := \mathbf{A}_{JJ} + \diag(\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{J'J}),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{diag}(.)$ embeds a vector into the diagonal of a matrix. This operation ensures that any probability mass that would have left $J$ (as governed by $\mathbf{A}_{J'J}$) is instantaneously reflected back to its state of origin, creating a reflecting boundary condition.

\begin{proposition}[Compressed generator properties]
\label{prop:compressed-generator}
The compressed generator $\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathbf{1}^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ} = \mathbf{0}^\top$ (zero column sums, hence mass conservation),
\item $\widetilde{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{y}} \geq 0$ for $\boldsymbol{x} \neq \boldsymbol{y}$ (non-negative off-diagonals),
\item $\widetilde{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}} \leq 0$ for all $\boldsymbol{x}$ (non-positive diagonal),
\item $e^{t\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}} \geq 0$ and $\|e^{t\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}}\|_{1 \to 1} = 1$ for all $t \geq 0$ (generates a positive stochastic semigroup).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Properties (1)-(3) follow directly from the construction of $\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}$. For property (1), using Eq.~\eqref{eq:row-sum-identities} and $\mathbf{1}^\top \diag(\boldsymbol{v}) = \boldsymbol{v}^\top$,
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{1}^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ} = \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ} + \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{J'J} = \mathbf{0}^\top.
\end{align*}
Property (2) holds because off-diagonal entries of $\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}$ equal those of $\mathbf{A}_{JJ}$, which are non-negative. For property (3), the diagonal entries are $\widetilde{A}_{xx} = A_{xx} + \sum_{y \in J'} A_{yx} = - \sum_{y \in J, y \neq x} A_{yx} \leq 0$. For property (4), we first establish the norm bound, then positivity. For any $\boldsymbol{p}(0) \geq \mathbf{0}$ with $\|\boldsymbol{p}(0)\|_1 = 1$, the solution $\boldsymbol{p}(t) = e^{t\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}}\boldsymbol{p}(0)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\|\boldsymbol{p}(t)\|_1 = \mathbf{1}^\top \frac{d\boldsymbol{p}}{dt} = \mathbf{1}^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}\boldsymbol{p}(t) = (\mathbf{1}^\top \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ})\boldsymbol{p}(t) = 0,
\end{equation}
by property (1). Therefore, $\|\boldsymbol{p}(t)\|_1 = \|\boldsymbol{p}(0)\|_1 = 1$ for all $t \geq 0$. Taking the supremum over all initial conditions yields $\|e^{t\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}}\|_{1 \to 1} = 1$. Next, we prove positivity: by properties (2)-(3), choose $\alpha \geq \max_{\boldsymbol{x}} |\widetilde{A}_{\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}}|$ large enough that $\mathbf{B} := \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ} + \alpha I$ has all non-negative entries. Then
\begin{align*}
e^{t\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}} &= e^{t(\mathbf{B} - \alpha I)} \\
                        &= e^{-\alpha t} e^{t\mathbf{B}} \\
                        &= e^{-\alpha t} \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(I + \frac{t}{n}\mathbf{B}\right)^n.
\end{align*}

Since $\mathbf{B} \geq 0$, each factor $I + \frac{t}{n}\mathbf{B} \geq 0$ for $t \geq 0$ and $n$ sufficiently large. Non-negative matrices are closed under multiplication, so $(I + \frac{t}{n}\mathbf{B})^n \geq 0$ for all $n$. Taking the limit and multiplying by the positive scalar $e^{-\alpha t} > 0$, we can conclude that $e^{t\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}} \geq 0$
\end{proof}
We now establish the contraction property for the truncated generator $\mathbf{A}_{JJ}$, which is needed for the local and global error bounds.

\begin{corollary}[Truncated generator contraction]
\label{cor:truncated-contraction}
The truncated generator $\mathbf{A}_{JJ}$ with non-positive column sums generates a positive contraction semigroup satisfying $e^{t\mathbf{A}_{JJ}} \geq 0$ and $\|e^{t\mathbf{A}_{JJ}}\|_{1 \to 1} \le 1$ for all $t \ge 0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For the norm bound, consider any $\boldsymbol{p}(0) \geq \mathbf{0}$ with $\|\boldsymbol{p}(0)\|_1 = 1$. Since $\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ} \leq \mathbf{0}^\top$, we have
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\|\boldsymbol{p}(t)\|_1 = \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ}\boldsymbol{p}(t) = (\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ})\boldsymbol{p}(t) \leq 0,
\end{equation}
which gives $\|e^{t\mathbf{A}_{JJ}}\|_{1 \to 1} \leq 1$. Positivity follows by the same shifting argument as in Proposition~\ref{prop:compressed-generator}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Infinite-dimensional extension]
\label{rem:infinite-dimensional}
The arguments in Proposition~\ref{prop:compressed-generator} and Corollary~\ref{cor:truncated-contraction} extend to the full CME on infinite state space $\mathcal{X}$ via the Hille-Yosida theorem~\cite{Ethier1986,Pazy1983}. In the infinite-dimensional setting, the operator $\mathbf{A}$ is unbounded on $\ell^1(\mathcal{X})$, requiring verification that $\mathbf{A}$ is closed with dense domain $D(\mathbf{A})$ and that the resolvent bounds $\|(\lambda I - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\|_{1 \to 1} \leq 1/\lambda$ hold for all $\lambda > 0$. These conditions guarantee that $\mathbf{A}$ generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup. In our finite-dimensional truncation to $J$, all operators are automatically bounded and closed with domain equal to the entire space, so the Hille-Yosida machinery reduces to the elementary arguments presented here.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Local Error}

We decompose the local error into model error from state space truncation and time-stepping error from numerical approximation.

\subsubsection{Model Error from State Space Truncation}

Consider the dynamics on $J$ starting from the true state $\boldsymbol{p}_J(t_n)$. The true restricted dynamics satisfy

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:true-restricted}
\frac{d}{dt} \boldsymbol{p}_J(t) = \mathbf{A}_{JJ}\boldsymbol{p}_J(t) + \mathbf{A}_{JJ'}\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t),
\end{equation}
while the compressed dynamics evolve according to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:compressed-dynamics-exact}
\frac{d}{dt} \widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_J(t) = \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_J(t).
\end{equation}
Define the local model error $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t) := \boldsymbol{p}_J(t) - \widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_J(t)$ with $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_n) = \mathbf{0}$.

\begin{theorem}[Local model error bound]
\label{thm:local-model-error}
Over a single time step $[t_n, t_{n+1}]$, the local model error satisfies
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1})\|_1 \leq \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} \left[\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(s) + \Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(s)\right] ds.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Subtracting Eq.~\eqref{eq:compressed-dynamics-exact} from Eq.~\eqref{eq:true-restricted} gives us the following inhomogenous equation,
\begin{align*}
\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t) &= \mathbf{A}_{JJ}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t) + \underbrace{\mathbf{A}_{JJ'}\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t) - \diag(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{A}_{J'J})\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_J(t)}_{=: \boldsymbol{f}(t)}.
\end{align*}
The solution of the inhomogeneous ODE is given by,
\begin{align}
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1}) &= \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} e^{(t_{n+1}-s)\mathbf{A}_{JJ}}\boldsymbol{f}(s) \, ds. \\
\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1})\|_1 &\leq \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} \underbrace{\|e^{(t_{n+1}-s)\mathbf{A}_{JJ}}\|_{1}}_{\leq 1 }\|\boldsymbol{f}(s)\|_1 \, ds \\
&\leq \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} (\|\mathbf{A}_{JJ'}\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(s)\|_1  + \|\diag(\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{A}_{J'J})\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_J(s)\|_1)  \; ds. \\
&= \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} ( (\mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{A}_{JJ'})\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(s) + (\mathbf{1}^\top\mathbf{A}_{J'J})\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_J(s)) \;ds \\
&= \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} (\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(s) + \Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(s)) \; ds.
\end{align}

\end{proof}


\subsubsection{Matrix Exponential Approximation Error}
\label{subsec:time_stepping_error_analysis}

In addition to state-space truncation, we approximate the matrix exponential $\exp(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}\,\Delta t)$ numerically. 

\begin{proposition}[Local time-stepping error]
\label{prop:local_time_error}
For a matrix exponential approximation with tolerance $\epsilon_{\mathrm{ODE}}$, the error in a single time step is bounded by
\begin{equation}
\|\exp(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}\,\Delta t)\,\boldsymbol{v} - \mathrm{Approx}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{JJ}, \Delta t)\,\boldsymbol{v}\|_1 \le \epsilon_{\mathrm{ODE}}\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_1.
\end{equation}
Modern Krylov subspace implementations such as EXPOKIT \cite{Sidje1998} provide adaptive mechanisms to control this error by adjusting the Krylov subspace dimension based on the specified tolerance.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Global Error}

\begin{corollary}[Global error bound]
\label{cor:global-error}
Let $e_n := \|\boldsymbol{p}(t_n) - \widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_n\|_1$ denote the global error, where $\widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_n$ is the numerical solution on $J$ extended with zeros. For $N$ time steps with $e_0 = 0$,
\begin{equation}
e_N \leq \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} (\varepsilon_n + \tau_n),
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_n := \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} (\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(s) + \Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(s)) \, ds$ is the local model error and $\tau_n := \|\widehat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n+1} - \widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n+1}\|_1$ is the time-stepping error.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The global error decomposes as $e_n = \|\boldsymbol{p}_J(t_n) - \widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_n\|_1 + \|\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t_n)\|_1$. By the triangle inequality,
\begin{align*}
\|\boldsymbol{p}_J(t_{n+1}) - \widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n+1}\|_1 &\leq \|\boldsymbol{p}_J(t_{n+1}) - e^{\Delta t_n \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{J_n}}\boldsymbol{p}_J(t_n)\|_1 \\
&\quad + \|e^{\Delta t_n \widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{J_n}}(\boldsymbol{p}_J(t_n) - \widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_n)\|_1 + \tau_n \\
&\leq \varepsilon_n + \|\boldsymbol{p}_J(t_n) - \widetilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_n\|_1 + \tau_n,
\end{align*}
where the first term follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:local-model-error} and the second uses Proposition~\ref{prop:compressed-generator}(4). For the $J'$ component, mass conservation gives
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t_{n+1})\|_1 = \|\boldsymbol{p}_{J'}(t_n)\|_1 + \int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} (\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(s) - \Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(s)) \, ds.
\end{equation}
Combining these bounds yields $e_{n+1} \leq e_n + \varepsilon_n + \tau_n$. Unrolling the recursion with $e_0 = 0$ gives the result.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Local error bound with pruning tolerances]
\label{cor:local-error-pruning}
Under pruning with probability tolerance $\alpha$ and flux tolerance $\epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}}$, the local error over $[t_n, t_{n+1}]$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1})\|_1 \leq \Delta t_n \left[\alpha + \epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}} \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_n = t_{n+1} - t_n$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n) = \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in J_n} \Phi(\boldsymbol{x}, t_n)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:local-model-error}, the error is bounded by $\int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}} [\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(s) + \Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(s)] ds$. The pruning algorithm ensures $\sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in J'} p(\boldsymbol{x}, t_n) \leq \alpha$, giving $\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(s) \lesssim \alpha$. Flux protection ensures $\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t_n) \leq \epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}} \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n)$. Integrating over $\Delta t_n$ yields the result.
\end{proof}

In the following corollaries we derive the connection between the error tolerances for the approximations in the proposed method and the theoretical error bounds derived previously. The tolerances introduced are - (i) $\alpha$ the total probability mass of the states to be removed, (ii) $\epsilon_{\text{flux}}$ tolorance for the magnitude of flux to be maintained and (iii) $\epsilon_{\text{time}}$, which is the allowed tolerance for scaling the time step according to the instantaneous flux.  
\begin{corollary}[Flux-adaptive time step]
\label{cor:flux-timestep}
To ensure the local model error satisfies $\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1})\|_1 \leq \epsilon_{\Delta t}$ for a given tolerance $\epsilon_{\Delta t} > 0$, choose
\begin{equation}
\Delta t_n = \frac{\epsilon_{\Delta t}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n)}, \qquad \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n) := \sum_{x \in J_n} p(x, t_n) w(x),
\end{equation}
where $w(x) = -A_{xx}$ is the total exit rate from state $x$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Assuming the fluxes remain approximately constant over $[t_n, t_{n+1}]$,
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1})\|_1 \approx [\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(t_n) + \Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t_n)] \Delta t_n.
\end{equation}
Since $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n)$ provides an upper bound on both $\Phi_{\mathrm{in}}(t_n)$ and $\Phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t_n)$, we have
\begin{equation}
\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(t_{n+1})\|_1 \lesssim 2\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n) \Delta t_n.
\end{equation}
Setting this equal to $\epsilon_{\Delta t}$ yields the stated time step.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Oscillatory Dynamics in the Oregonator Model}

We examine the Oregonator model, a reduced representation of the Belousov-Zhabotinsky oscillating chemical reaction developed by Field and Noyes \cite{field_noyes}. This three-species system captures the essential autocatalytic and inhibitory feedback mechanisms that generate sustained chemical oscillations. The Oregonator serves as a stringent test for numerical methods due to its combination of multiple timescales, relaxation oscillations, and sensitivity to parameter variations. The Oregonator consists of five elementary reactions involving three intermediate species: $X$ (HBrO$_2$), $Y$ (Br$^-$), and $Z$ (Ce$^{4+}$). The complete reaction network is presented in Table \ref{table:oregonator}. The reaction network exhibits three distinct dynamical phases. The autocatalytic reaction (R3) drives explosive growth of $X$ when inhibitor $Y$ is depleted. The quadratic termination (R4) prevents unbounded growth at high $X$ concentrations. The recovery reaction (R5) slowly regenerates $Y$ from $Z$, resetting the cycle. Following Gillespie's methodology \cite{Gillespie1977}, we determine rate constants by analyzing the deterministic steady state. At equilibrium, the net flux for each species vanishes:

\begin{align}
\frac{dN_X}{dt} &= k_1 N_Y - k_2 N_X N_Y + k_3 N_X - 2k_4 N_X^2 = 0, \\
\frac{dN_Y}{dt} &= -k_1 N_Y - k_2 N_X N_Y + k_5 N_Z = 0, \\
\frac{dN_Z}{dt} &= k_3 N_X - k_5 N_Z = 0.
\end{align}

We specify the steady-state populations as $(N_X^*, N_Y^*, N_Z^*) = (500, 1000, 2000)$ molecules, placing the system in the oscillatory regime. From the third equation:
\begin{equation}
k_3 N_X^* = k_5 N_Z^* \implies \mu_3 = \mu_5,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i = k_i N_i^*$ denotes the steady-state flux through reaction $i$. Defining the characteristic fluxes $\mu_1^* = k_1 N_Y^* = 2000$ s$^{-1}$ and $\mu_2^* = k_2 N_X^* N_Y^* = 50000$ s$^{-1}$, we solve for the rate constants as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:oregonator_rates}. These parameters yield a period of approximately 3.3 time units and amplitude variations spanning two orders of magnitude in species $X$.

\subsection{Stiff Dynamics in the Robertson Autocatalytic System}

The Robertson system provides a severe test of numerical methods through its extreme stiffness, with reaction rates spanning nine orders of magnitude. Originally formulated by Robertson \cite{Robertson1967} for testing ODE solvers, this autocatalytic network has become a standard benchmark for stochastic simulation algorithms. The system involves three species undergoing the reactions shown in Table \ref{table:robertson}. The stiffness emerges from the rate constant hierarchy: $k_1 = 0.04$ s$^{-1}$, $k_2 = 3 \times 10^7$ molecule$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$, and $k_3 = 10^4$ molecule$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$, yielding a stiffness ratio of $k_2/k_1 \approx 10^9$.

\caption{Robertson autocatalytic reaction system}
\label{table:robertson}
\begin{tabular}{|c|l|l|l|}

\hline
1 & $A \xrightarrow{k_1} B$ & $k_1 N_A$ & Slow initiation \\
2 & $2B \xrightarrow{k_2} B + C$ & $k_2 N_B(N_B-1)/2$ & Fast autocatalysis \\

\end{table}

The system conserves total mass: $N_{\text{tot}} = N_A + N_B + N_C = 10000$ molecules. We initialize with $(N_A, N_B, N_C) = (10000, 0, 0)$, triggering the characteristic three-phase dynamics visible in Figure \ref{fig:robertson_results}. The system evolves through three distinct phases, each presenting unique numerical challenges as shown in table~\ref{table:robertson}. The Robertson system evolution, depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:robertson_results}, exhibits three distinct dynamical regimes that test the limits of numerical methods. During the initial phase ($t < 10^{-2}$ s), species $A$ undergoes slow conversion to $B$ through reaction R1 at a rate of $0.04$ s$^{-1}$. Throughout this period, the algorithm maintains time steps of approximately $10^{-4}$ s while the state space remains minimal, containing fewer than 50 states. The flux hovers around 400 events per second, reflecting the sluggish kinetics. This phase concludes when species $B$ accumulates to approximately 30 molecules, crossing the critical threshold for autocatalytic activation.

The system then enters its most transient phase between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-2}$ seconds, where the autocatalytic reaction R2 fires rapidly, resulting in explosive dynamics. Once activated, species $B$ undergoes rapid amplification, surging from 30 molecules to a peak of approximately $3 \times 10^3$ molecules near $t = 1$ s, before being consumed to produce $C$. The algorithm's response to this stiff transient reveals the interplay between flux and computational requirements. The time step contracts by three orders of magnitude to $10^{-5}$ s, necessary to accurately resolve the rapid concentration changes. Simultaneously, the state space expands dramatically to over 1400 states as the probability distribution broadens due to increased stochastic fluctuations during the burst. Following the transient, the system enters a protracted equilibration phase for $t > 10^2$ s. Reaction R3 slowly regenerates species $A$ while maintaining $B$ at trace concentrations, gradually approaching the equilibrium of $(N_A, N_B, N_C) \approx (0, 5, 10000)$ molecules. The algorithm exploits the reduced activity to improve efficiency: time steps increase progressively to $10^3$ s, spanning seven orders of magnitude from their minimum value, while the state space contracts to approximately 300 states as the probability distribution concentrates around the equilibrium manifold. The flux decreases to near-baseline levels, confirming the system's approach to steady state. This adaptive behavior demonstrates how flux-based control enables efficient navigation of systems with extreme temporal scale separation, automatically identifying and responding to changes in dynamical regime without prior knowledge or manual intervention.


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We presented an adaptive FSP approach for the CME that combines a flux-based pruning heuristic with a flux-adaptive time-step controller. The method is designed to preserve network connectivity while adjusting the temporal resolution to the current activity level. We established global error bounds for the resulting scheme and evaluated the approach on benchmark systems exhibiting stiffness and metastability. The experiments indicate that the method achieves good accuracy at competitive computational cost and remains stable on problems where probability-only pruning is fragile.

This work has limitations. Our analysis focuses on additive bounds that separate truncation and time-stepping contributions; sharper problem-dependent constants and tighter estimates for specific reaction classes remain to be developed. Moreover, the present implementation relies on standard exponential actions; alternative solvers and preconditioners may further improve efficiency. Future directions include adaptive control that couples flux with moment or observable targets, extensions to parameter inference and sensitivity analysis, and applications to higher-dimensional networks where truncation design is especially challenging.
