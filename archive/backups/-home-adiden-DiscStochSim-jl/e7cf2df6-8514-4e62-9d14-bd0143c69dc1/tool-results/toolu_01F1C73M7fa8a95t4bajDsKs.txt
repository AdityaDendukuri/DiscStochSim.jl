46-\section{Introduction}
47-The Chemical Master Equation (CME) governs the evolution of probability distributions for well-mixed stochastic reaction networks and is the canonical description of intrinsic noise in biochemical systems \cite{Gillespie1977, Wilkinson2006-so}. 
48-Formally, the CME is an infinite system of linear ODEs posed on a countably infinite lattice of molecular population states. 
49-Because the reachable state space typically grows combinatorially with copy numbers, the CME cannot be solved directly without an adaptive or carefully structured truncation scheme.

55-\subsection{Related Work}
56-\label{sec:related-work}
57-We review prior work on adaptive FSP methods and state space truncation strategies for stochastic chemical kinetics. The original FSP method \cite{Munsky2006} introduced fixed truncation with rigorous error bounds based on the probability flux leaving the truncated domain. Munsky and Khammash \cite{MUNSKY2007818} extended this framework to time-stepping variants that repeatedly expand the state space along reachable directions, providing adaptive control of the truncation error over time.

71-\subsection{The Chemical Master Equation}
72-
73-\begin{definition}[Chemical Master Equation (CME)]
74-The \textit{Chemical Master Equation (CME)} describes the time evolution of the probability distribution $p(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$ over the state $\boldsymbol{x}$. For a single state $\boldsymbol{x}$, the CME is
75-\begin{equation}
76-    \frac{d}{dt} p(\boldsymbol{x}, t) =
77-    \sum_{k=1}^{M} \big[\alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\nu}_k)\, p(\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\nu}_k, t)
78-    - \alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x})\, p(\boldsymbol{x}, t)\big],
--
151-  \STATE \textbf{Assemble:} build $\mathbf{A}_{JJ}$ on $J$
152-  \STATE \textbf{Evolve:} $\boldsymbol{p} \gets \exp(\mathbf{A}_{JJ}\Delta t)\,\boldsymbol{p}$
153-  \STATE \textbf{Prune:} remove states with $p(x)$ below a heuristic threshold
154-  \STATE $t \gets \min\{t+\Delta t,\,t_f\}$
155-\ENDWHILE
156-\STATE \textbf{return} $(J,\boldsymbol{p})$ at $t_f$
157-\end{algorithmic}
158-\end{algorithm}
159-
160-This \emph{sliding-window} approach provides a general template for adaptive FSP methods with dynamically evolving state spaces \cite{Dinh2016}. Many variants differ in how expansion and pruning are performed, but they all follow the same broad framework which is to enlarge the state space to capture new probability flow, evolve the distribution on the truncated generator, and prune regions that have become irrelevant. 
161-The remainder of this paper focuses on refining and analyzing the pruning and time step determination steps within this general adaptive FSP skeleton.

246-\section{Methodology: Flux-Based Adaptive FSP}
247-\label{sec:methodology}
248-
249-This section introduces a state space adaptation approach that preserves network connectivity through flux-aware truncation. We first define and characterize probability flux as a connectivity measure, argue that naive probability-based pruning can destroy essential pathways, then construct an algorithm that maintains structural integrity of the state-space graph while controlling computational cost.
250-
251-\begin{definition}[Outgoing probability flux]
252-\label{def:flux}
253-Let $w(\boldsymbol{x}) := \sum_{k=1}^M \alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x}) = -\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}}$ denote the total exit rate from state $\boldsymbol{x}$. The \textit{outgoing probability flux} from state $\boldsymbol{x}$ at time $t$ is
254-\begin{equation}
255-\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},t) := p(\boldsymbol{x},t) \cdot w(\boldsymbol{x}).
256-\end{equation}
--
282-Since the diagonal entries $\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}} = -\sum_{k=1}^M \alpha_k(\boldsymbol{x})$ depend only on the reaction network structure and state $\boldsymbol{x}$, the total flux evolves with the probability distribution. 
283-\end{proof}

304-\subsubsection{Flux-Aware Truncation Algorithm}
305-
306-We propose a two-stage pruning approach that combines probability-based candidate selection with flux-based protection. The algorithm identifies states for potential removal using a quantile threshold, then protects low-probability states that carry significant probability flow.
307-
308-\begin{algorithm}[H]
309-\caption{Flux-Preserving Pruning}
310-\label{alg:flux-prune}
311-\begin{algorithmic}[1]
312-\REQUIRE Active set $\mathcal{S}$, probability vector $\boldsymbol{p}$, generator $\mathbf{A}$, quantile tolerance $\alpha \in (0,1)$, flux tolerance $\epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}} > 0$
--
323-    \STATE Compute exit rate: $w(\boldsymbol{x}) = -\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}}$
324-    \STATE Compute flux: $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}, t) = p(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \cdot w(\boldsymbol{x})$
325-\ENDFOR
326-\STATE Compute total flux: $\Phi_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{S}} \Phi(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$
327-\STATE Set flux threshold: $\Phi_{\mathrm{thr}} = \epsilon_{\mathrm{flux}} \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}$
328-\STATE Define protected set: $\mathcal{P} = \{\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathcal{C} : \Phi(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \geq \Phi_{\mathrm{thr}}\}$
329-\STATE Define pruning set: $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{prune}} = \mathcal{C} \setminus \mathcal{P}$
330-\STATE
331-\STATE \textit{Stage 3: Update and Renormalization}
332-\STATE $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \mathcal{S} \setminus \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{prune}}$
333-\STATE $\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{new}} \gets \boldsymbol{p}|_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}} / \|\boldsymbol{p}|_{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}}\|_1$ \quad (restrict and renormalize)
334-\RETURN $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}, \boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{new}}$
335-\end{algorithmic}
336-\end{algorithm}

342-[Omitted long context line]
343-\begin{equation}
344-\label{eq:flux-timestep}
345-\Delta t_n = \frac{\epsilon_{\Delta t}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(\mathcal{S}_n, t_n)},
346-\end{equation}
347-where $\epsilon_{\Delta t}$ is a dimensionless tolerance parameter. This rule ensures that $\Delta t_n \cdot \Phi_{\mathrm{total}}(t_n) = \epsilon_{\Delta t}$, bounding the total probability mass that can cross the truncation boundary during the step. The formal justification follows from the local error analysis in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

352-
353-[Omitted long context line]

403-\WHILE{$t < t_f$}
404-  \STATE \textbf{Expand:} $\mathcal{S}_{\text{new}} \gets \mathcal{S} \cup \partial \mathcal{S}$ (expand state space)
405-  \STATE \textbf{Construct:} $\mathbf{A}_{\text{new}} \gets$ forward enumeration (Algorithm~\ref{alg:matrix_construction})

418:The computational bottleneck is the matrix exponential action $\exp(\mathbf{A}_{\text{new}}\Delta t)\boldsymbol{p}$ in the evolution step. We employ Krylov subspace methods via the EXPOKIT package \cite{Sidje1998}, which computes matrix exponential actions without forming the full exponential matrix. For a sparse generator $\mathbf{A}$ of dimension $n = |\mathcal{S}|$ with
419-$\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}) = O(n)$, EXPOKIT's Krylov--Arnoldi routine for
420-computing $e^{t\mathbf{A}} v$ has per-step cost
421-\[
422-  O\bigl(m\,\mathrm{nnz}(\mathbf{A}) + m^2 n\bigr) = O(m^2 n),
423-\]
424-where $m \ll n$ is the Krylov subspace dimension. In our setting, the
425-expansion and pruning operations scale as $O(nR)$, and constructing
426-$\mathbf{A}$ by forward enumeration also costs $O(nR)$, where $R$ is the
427-number of reactions. Since $m$ and $R$ are small constants independent of
428-$n$, the total per-step cost is effectively linear in the state-space size,
429-making simulations with $10^4$--$10^5$ states computationally tractable.

720-
721-We consider a three-species network with a slow gateway followed by fast downstream dynamics:
722-\begin{equation}
723-A \xrightarrow{k_1} B, \qquad B \xrightarrow{k_2} B + C,
724-\end{equation}
725:with state $(N_A,N_B,N_C)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^3$, $k_1=10^{-6}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, $k_2=1\,\text{s}^{-1}$, and initial condition $(1,0,0)$. The slow $A \to B$ transition acts as a bottleneck: once probability reaches states with $B \geq 1$, the fast catalytic reaction rapidly advances $C$ while maintaining $B \approx 1$.
